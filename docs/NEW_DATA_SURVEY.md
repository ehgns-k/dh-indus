# Survey of unused data: new Indus inscriptions and other machine-readable datasets

Compiled 25 November 2026.

## 1. Introduction

The project's main corpus is an ICIT-style transcription with 5,679 records (`data/raw/icit2025.csv`). It was downloaded from https://github.com/horus84/Computational_Linguistics/blob/ac44f08c835de3b709875441eea9d60d1811e3cf/data/inscriptions.csv. A fresh download of that file at the pinned commit matches the local copy exactly: every `(id, text)` pair is the same. To confirm any finding statistically, the project needs inscriptions that this corpus does not contain. This survey looks for two kinds of such data.

1. Inscriptions published from about 2010 to 2026 whose sign sequence can be read from a published photograph, a drawing, a published reading or a dataset.
2. Machine-readable datasets of Indus inscriptions other than the ICIT-style CSV and the Koskenniemi–Parpola (1979) corpus.

**How the survey was done.** Web pages could not be opened from the working environment. academia.edu, rarebooksocietyofindia.org, harappa.com, arxiv.org, zenodo.org, hal.science, nature.com, wikipedia.org, indus.epigraphica.de and indusscript.in all failed to resolve. Statements about publications therefore rest on search-engine excerpts, and the primary papers were not read. The exception is GitHub: repositories were cloned and their files inspected directly, so statements about their contents, sizes and licences come from the files themselves.

**What "in the corpus" means.** A site's presence was checked by counting its records in `icit2025.csv`. Whether a particular object is in the corpus was usually not decided, because most publications give no CISI number or registration. Several third-party repositories have tried to match published objects against an ICIT copy. Their results are cited as theirs. They were not checked against the primary sources.

**Transcribability rating.** The last column of each table says whether the sign sequences could realistically be put into ICIT/Wells numbering by this project:

- **yes**: a legible image or a published reading in a numbered sign list is available.
- **partly**: some signs are legible, the image is poor, or the reading exists only in another numbering or in words.
- **no**: no image or reading is accessible.

Corpus record counts per site, from `icit2025.csv`, are quoted as "corpus n = ...".

## 4. Conclusions: most promising sources of unused data

1. **Surkotada potsherds (Joshi 1990, MASI 87, on archive.org).** This is the largest accessible set of inscriptions with photographs that the corpus seems to lack: 23 objects against 6 corpus records. It falls before 2010 but is openly downloadable, so it can realistically be transcribed (**yes**).
2. **Tell Jokha (Umma) seal fragment (2017) and the two Binjor (4MSR) seals (2015–16).** These are clearly outside the corpus. Readings in Wells or Mahadevan numbering already exist in third-party repositories, but they are short and partly uncertain (**partly**). The primary reports should be obtained before use.
3. **Bisht 2015 Dholavira report.** It holds up to 10 seals that may be new, plus pottery inscriptions published by Vinod. Each candidate must first be checked against the 238 Dholavira records of the corpus, because most of the report is already in the corpus (**partly**).
4. **Single objects in ASI and journal publications.** These are Gola Dhoro (Jamison et al.), Lakhanjo-daro (Biagi & Vidale 2022), Dhalewan (ASI Memoir 2018), Bhirrana and Rupar (IAR), and Khirsara (IAR 2009–11). Each adds one to three objects. Their readings need the original plates.
5. **Datasets.** No machine-readable dataset was found that adds more than a handful of objects to the ICIT-style CSV. The current Lipi export adds one object, a Dilmun seal. The k-sandhu inventory is the best finding aid for new objects, but it contains no signs. The other repositories are subsets of ICIT, of Mahadevan 1977 or of CISI.

In total, the unused data realistically within reach is small: a few dozen inscriptions, most of them short, with Surkotada the largest group. That is probably too little for a well-powered confirmatory test. It is enough for a small qualitative check, and for a check of predictions made before transcription.
